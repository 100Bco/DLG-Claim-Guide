## What "burden of proof" means

The [burden of proof](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/burden_of_proof) is the responsibility a party carries to prove a disputed fact or claim to the required degree. It answers two related questions: who must prove something, and how convincingly. In a civil lawsuit, the party bringing the claim — the plaintiff — generally bears the initial burden of establishing the elements of the claim.

The concept has two components. The burden of production is the obligation to come forward with enough evidence for an issue to be considered at all. The burden of persuasion is the obligation to actually convince the finder of fact — the jury, or the judge in a bench trial — that the claim is established to the applicable standard. If a party who carries the burden fails to meet it, the claim on that issue generally fails.

Placing the burden on the plaintiff reflects a basic principle: the party asserting that something happened is generally the one asked to prove it, rather than requiring the other side to disprove it.

## The civil standard: preponderance of the evidence

Most civil claims are decided under the [preponderance of the evidence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/preponderance_of_the_evidence) standard. This means the party with the burden must show that the disputed fact is more likely true than not — often described as tipping the scale just past the halfway point, or greater than a 50 percent likelihood. If the evidence is evenly balanced, the party carrying the burden has not met it.

This is a meaningfully lower threshold than the criminal standard. In a criminal prosecution, the government must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, a much higher level of certainty. The difference reflects what is at stake: criminal cases can result in loss of liberty, while civil cases generally involve money or other non-criminal remedies.

## A higher intermediate standard for some issues

Not every civil issue uses the preponderance standard. Certain matters call for clear and convincing evidence, an intermediate standard that requires a higher degree of certainty than preponderance but less than the criminal standard. It is sometimes described as showing that a fact is highly probable.

- **Preponderance of the evidence** — the usual civil standard; more likely than not.
- **Clear and convincing evidence** — a heightened standard used for particular issues, such as some claims involving fraud or, in many states, the availability of punitive damages.
- **Beyond a reasonable doubt** — the criminal standard, generally not used in civil cases.

Which standard applies to a given issue is a question of law that can vary by jurisdiction and by the type of claim.

## How the burden can shift and vary by state

Although the plaintiff usually starts with the burden, it does not always remain fixed. When a defendant raises an affirmative defense — a defense that introduces new facts, such as consent or the expiration of the statute of limitations — the defendant generally bears the burden of proving that defense. In this way, different issues within the same case can be assigned to different parties.

The details are governed by rules of evidence and procedure that differ by jurisdiction. States set their own standards for particular claims, their own rules on when burdens shift, and their own treatment of presumptions that can affect what must be proven. Because these rules vary, the general framework described here should be confirmed against the law of the relevant court.

## Presumptions and inferences

The burden of proof does not operate in isolation; it interacts with legal tools that can affect what a party must show. A presumption is a rule that requires the finder of fact to assume a particular fact once certain other facts are established, unless the opposing party produces evidence to rebut it. Presumptions can shift the burden of producing evidence, though they do not always shift the ultimate burden of persuasion.

In some negligence contexts, courts recognize an inference known as res ipsa loquitur — a Latin phrase meaning "the thing speaks for itself." Where it applies, the circumstances of an injury are such that negligence may be inferred even without direct evidence of exactly what went wrong, provided the recognized conditions are met. It is generally treated as a way of establishing enough evidence to reach the finder of fact, not as an automatic finding of liability.

- **Presumption** — assumes a fact once predicate facts are shown, subject to rebuttal.
- **Inference** — permits, but does not require, the finder of fact to conclude a fact from the evidence.

How presumptions and inferences work, and their precise effect on the burden, is governed by each jurisdiction's rules of evidence and case law. As a result, whether a particular presumption applies, and what it takes to rebut it, can vary by state. These doctrines illustrate that the burden of proof is a framework applied alongside other rules rather than a single, mechanical test.

## Why the standard matters to the outcome

The burden of proof shapes how a case is argued and decided. Because the civil plaintiff need only show that a claim is more likely true than not, civil liability can attach even where the same conduct would not support a criminal conviction. This is one reason a defendant may be found not guilty in a criminal case yet still be held liable in a related civil case arising from the same events.

Understanding who must prove what, and to what degree, clarifies why civil disputes so often turn on the weight and credibility of the evidence rather than on absolute certainty.
