## The core concept

Negligence is a legal theory that holds a person or entity responsible for harm caused by a failure to exercise reasonable care. It does not require an intention to cause harm; it centers on conduct that falls short of what a reasonably careful person would do under similar circumstances. Negligence is the foundation of most personal injury claims, from ordinary accidents to many professional and premises disputes.

The reasonable-person standard is central to the idea. Rather than asking what a particular individual believed was acceptable, the law generally asks what a hypothetical, reasonably prudent person would have done in the same situation. This objective measure allows conduct to be judged against a shared benchmark rather than personal opinion.

Because negligence is defined largely through court decisions and long-standing common-law principles — reflected in sources such as the Restatement of Torts — its precise contours can differ across jurisdictions even though the general framework is widely shared.

## The four elements

To establish negligence, a plaintiff generally must prove four elements. All are typically required; the absence of any one can defeat the claim:

- **Duty** — the defendant owed the plaintiff a legal obligation to exercise a certain level of care.
- **Breach** — the defendant failed to meet that obligation through action or inaction.
- **Causation** — the breach actually and proximately caused the plaintiff's harm.
- **Damages** — the plaintiff suffered a recognized, compensable injury or loss.

Each element carries its own analysis, and disputes often focus on just one or two of them, such as whether a duty existed or whether the breach truly caused the injury.

## Duty and breach in more detail

The first question is whether the defendant owed the plaintiff a duty of care. Duties can arise from general obligations — such as the duty of a driver to operate a vehicle carefully — or from specific relationships and circumstances recognized by law. Whether a duty exists is often treated as a question for the court.

Breach asks whether the defendant's conduct fell below the required standard. This frequently turns on the facts: what the defendant did, what a reasonable person would have done, and what risks were foreseeable. In some situations, a violation of a safety statute can serve as evidence of breach, a concept sometimes called negligence per se, though its application varies by jurisdiction.

## Causation, defenses, and variation by state

Causation links the breach to the harm and typically has two parts. Actual cause asks whether the injury would have occurred but for the defendant's conduct. Proximate cause asks whether the harm was a sufficiently foreseeable result of that conduct, rather than a remote or freakish consequence. Both are generally required, and proximate cause in particular can be a contested, fact-specific issue.

Negligence claims are also subject to defenses and limits that vary by state. A plaintiff's own carelessness may reduce or bar recovery under comparative or contributory fault rules, which differ substantially by jurisdiction. Some defendants may be shielded by doctrines such as assumption of risk or by statutory protections in particular contexts. Because the elements and defenses are shaped by each state's statutes and case law, the general description here should be read as a framework rather than a uniform rule.

## Negligence and related standards

Ordinary negligence sits alongside several related standards of conduct, and distinguishing them helps clarify what a claim actually alleges. These variations can affect available defenses and, in some cases, the types of damages at issue.

- **Gross negligence** — conduct reflecting a greater departure from reasonable care than ordinary negligence, sometimes described as a reckless disregard for safety. In many states it can matter for issues such as the availability of certain damages or the effect of liability waivers.
- **Negligence per se** — a doctrine under which violating a safety statute designed to protect a class of people may establish the standard of care or serve as evidence of breach, subject to conditions that vary by jurisdiction.
- **Recklessness and intentional conduct** — states of mind beyond carelessness, which fall outside ordinary negligence and are analyzed under different rules.
- **Strict liability** — a separate basis for responsibility in limited areas, such as certain product or abnormally dangerous activity claims, where fault in the negligence sense may not need to be shown.

These categories are not interchangeable, and a single set of facts may support more than one theory. Which standard applies affects how a claim is framed and what must be proven. Because the definitions and consequences of gross negligence, negligence per se, and strict liability are set by each state's statutes and case law, their boundaries vary by jurisdiction, and the general descriptions here outline the concepts rather than any single state's rule.

## Why the framework matters

The four-element structure gives personal injury claims a predictable shape. It explains why establishing that an accident happened is not enough on its own: a plaintiff must connect a specific failure of care to a specific, compensable harm. It also explains why many disputes narrow quickly to a single question, such as whether a duty existed or whether the defendant's conduct was truly the cause of the injury.

Understanding negligence clarifies the logic behind much of personal injury law, but how the doctrine applies in any given case depends on the facts and on the law of the jurisdiction where the claim is brought.
