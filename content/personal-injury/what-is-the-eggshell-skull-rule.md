## What the eggshell skull rule means

The [eggshell skull rule](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/eggshell_skull_rule) is a long-standing principle of tort law captured by the phrase that a wrongdoer "takes the victim as they find them." Under this rule, a defendant whose negligence injures someone is generally liable for the full extent of the resulting harm, even when a pre-existing condition or unusual vulnerability caused the injury to be much more severe than it would have been for an average person. The name comes from the image of a person with a skull as thin as an eggshell: a blow that would merely bruise most people might cause that person a catastrophic injury, and the rule says the defendant is still responsible for the actual harm done.

The principle is sometimes called the "thin skull rule," and it reflects a basic policy judgment. Between an innocent injured person and the party whose wrongful conduct caused the harm, the law generally places the risk of unforeseen severity on the wrongdoer rather than on the victim. A defendant does not get a discount simply because the person they harmed turned out to be more fragile than expected.

Importantly, the rule addresses the extent of the damages, not the question of fault. It assumes the defendant is already liable for having negligently caused some injury; the rule then governs how far that liability extends when the consequences are unexpectedly serious.

## Where the rule comes from

The idea that a defendant answers for the full consequences of a wrongful act has deep roots in the common law. A frequently cited early example is the historical case *Vosburg v. Putney*, in which a schoolboy's seemingly minor kick aggravated a pre-existing condition in another boy's leg and led to serious, lasting harm; the court held the wrongdoer responsible for the full injury even though its severity was not foreseeable. The case is often used to illustrate the principle that unexpected extent of harm does not excuse an intentional or wrongful act.

The rule has since been reflected in influential summaries of tort law. The American Law Institute's Restatement (Second) of Torts, in provisions addressing the extent of liability for physical harm, expresses the idea that a negligent actor may be liable for harm that is increased by the other person's pre-existing physical condition. Over time, courts across jurisdictions have applied the principle broadly, treating it as a settled feature of how damages are measured once negligence is established.

## How the rule works in practice

In a typical case, the eggshell skull rule comes into play when a defendant argues that the plaintiff's injuries are unusually severe because of some prior vulnerability — an earlier back injury, a degenerative disease, a bleeding disorder, or a psychological predisposition, for example. The rule prevents the defendant from escaping responsibility for that added severity solely because it was unforeseeable.

Consider the general pattern:

- **A minor collision aggravates a prior condition** — a relatively low-speed crash worsens a plaintiff's pre-existing spinal condition, producing far greater harm than a healthy person would have suffered.
- **An ordinary impact triggers an outsized injury** — a fall causes severe bleeding in a person with a clotting disorder, or a modest blow fractures unusually brittle bones.
- **A distressing event provokes a severe psychological response** — in states that recognize it, an incident causes a disproportionate emotional injury because of a pre-existing susceptibility.

In each situation, the defendant generally remains liable for the actual harm caused, not merely the harm an average person would have experienced. The plaintiff still must prove that the defendant's negligence caused the injury; the rule then extends the measure of damages to the full, actual consequences.

## The rule and pre-existing conditions

A common point of confusion is how the eggshell skull rule interacts with a plaintiff's pre-existing condition. The rule does not make a defendant responsible for the pre-existing condition itself, only for the additional harm the negligence caused — including any aggravation of that condition. In practice, this often means separating the harm the plaintiff would have experienced anyway from the harm the defendant's conduct added.

This is why medical evidence tends to be central in these cases. Courts and juries frequently must distinguish between the natural course of a prior condition and the worsening attributable to the incident. The defendant remains responsible for the aggravation and for injuries that would not have occurred but for the negligence, even where their severity was surprising. The line between a pre-existing condition and its aggravation is a factual question, and how it is drawn can significantly affect the damages awarded.

## How the rule can vary by state

Although the eggshell skull rule is widely accepted in principle — and general [public legal-education materials](https://www.americanbar.org/groups/public_education/) describe it as a settled feature of tort law — its application is a matter of state law and can differ across jurisdictions. States may vary in how they instruct juries on the rule, how they treat the aggravation of pre-existing conditions, and how far the principle extends to purely psychological or emotional injuries, which some states limit more tightly than physical ones. The rule also interacts with other doctrines, such as [proximate cause](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/proximate_cause), which addresses whether a particular harm is close enough to the wrongful act to support liability at all.

The rule can further intersect with comparative fault, since a plaintiff's own share of responsibility for an accident may reduce recovery under a state's rules even where the eggshell skull principle governs the extent of the injury. Because these related doctrines and the precise jury instructions differ by state, the general description here should be confirmed against the law of the relevant jurisdiction.

## Why the rule matters

The eggshell skull rule matters because it shapes how the law allocates the risk of unexpected harm. By holding a wrongdoer responsible for the actual consequences of their conduct, rather than only the average or foreseeable consequences, the rule aligns liability with the real-world effects of negligence and protects people who happen to be more vulnerable than most.

At the same time, the rule is not a shortcut to a larger recovery. A plaintiff still must establish negligence and prove that the defendant's conduct caused the claimed harm, and defendants remain free to contest how much of an injury was actually caused by the incident as opposed to a pre-existing condition. Because the doctrine operates within each state's broader framework of negligence and damages, how it applies in any particular case depends on the facts and on the governing jurisdiction.
