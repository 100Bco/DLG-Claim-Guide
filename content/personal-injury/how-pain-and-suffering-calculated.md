## Why pain and suffering is hard to quantify

"Pain and suffering" is a form of non-economic damages — compensation for intangible harm rather than measurable financial loss. It can encompass physical pain, emotional distress, and a diminished ability to enjoy daily life. Unlike a medical bill or a lost paycheck, these harms do not come with a dollar amount attached, which makes them inherently difficult to measure.

Because there is no receipt for suffering, the law does not supply a precise equation to convert it into money. Instead, the value of these damages is generally treated as a question for the finder of fact — the jury, or the judge in a bench trial — to decide after considering the evidence. That evidence may include testimony about the injury, its effects on the person's life, and the expected course of recovery.

This structure means that two cases with similar injuries can produce different results. The subjectivity is not a flaw to be corrected with a formula; it reflects the reality that intangible harms differ from person to person and situation to situation.

## There is no authoritative formula

Discussions of pain and suffering sometimes reference informal methods, but it is important to understand that these are not legal rules and carry no binding authority:

- **The "multiplier" idea** — the notion of taking economic damages and applying some multiple is a negotiating heuristic that some parties may reference, not a standard set by statute or courts.
- **The "per diem" idea** — assigning a daily figure to suffering over a period of time is likewise an argument technique, not a legal requirement.

No court or statute mandates any such method, and a jury is generally not instructed to apply one. Presenting these approaches as authoritative would misstate how the law works. In practice, they are simply ways parties sometimes frame arguments during negotiation, and a finder of fact remains free to reach its own conclusion from the evidence.

## What influences the assessment

Although no formula governs, certain factors commonly inform how pain and suffering is evaluated by juries and in negotiations. These may include the nature and severity of the injury, the length and difficulty of recovery, whether the effects are expected to be permanent, and how credibly the impact is documented and described. The strength of the overall evidence on liability can also affect willingness to pay, since a contested case carries more risk for both sides.

Because these considerations are weighed rather than calculated, estimates from different observers can diverge substantially, and any prediction of a specific outcome is uncertain.

## Limits and variation by state

How non-economic damages like pain and suffering are handled differs by jurisdiction, and this is a significant source of variation. Some states impose statutory caps that limit the amount recoverable for non-economic damages, frequently in specific categories such as medical malpractice, while other states impose no cap or have found caps to conflict with their constitutions. Whether a limit applies depends entirely on the state and the type of claim.

States also differ in procedural rules that can affect these awards, including how comparative fault reduces a recovery and how courts review awards thought to be excessive. Because of this patchwork, the general description here should not be treated as a uniform national rule, and the governing law of the relevant jurisdiction controls.

## The role of evidence and testimony

Because pain and suffering cannot be documented like a bill, its assessment depends heavily on evidence that describes the injury's effects. Several kinds of evidence commonly play a part, and their strength tends to influence how the harm is evaluated:

- **Medical records** — documentation of diagnoses, treatment, and the expected course of recovery, which can corroborate the existence and severity of an injury.
- **Testimony from treating professionals** — descriptions of the injury and its likely effects from those who provided care.
- **Testimony from the injured person** — an account of how the injury has changed daily activities, work, and routines.
- **Lay witness testimony** — observations from family members, friends, or coworkers who can describe changes they have seen.

Credibility often matters as much as the content of the evidence. Consistency between what is described and what the records show can strengthen a claim, while gaps or contradictions can weaken it. A finder of fact remains free to weigh all of this and reach its own conclusion, since no rule converts the evidence into a set figure.

The nature and permanence of an injury can also shape the assessment. Harms expected to be long-lasting are generally described and documented differently than those expected to resolve. Because these evaluations rest on judgment rather than calculation, and because state rules on evidence and on limits to non-economic damages differ, outcomes can vary considerably even in cases that appear similar.

## How these damages fit a claim

In most personal injury claims, pain and suffering is one component of the overall damages, alongside economic losses such as medical costs and lost income. During settlement talks, the parties often discuss it as part of a single negotiated figure rather than a separately itemized amount, and at trial a jury may be asked to assign a value as part of its verdict.

The central point is that pain and suffering is assessed, not computed. It rests on evidence and judgment, is shaped by state-specific limits, and does not lend itself to any fixed number that applies across cases.
