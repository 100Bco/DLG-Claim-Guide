## What a demand letter is

A demand letter is a formal written statement in which one party sets out a claim against another and asks for a specific response, most often payment of a sum of money. In the personal injury context, it is typically sent by an injured person, or an attorney on their behalf, to the party alleged to be responsible or to that party's insurance company.

The letter serves as an organized presentation of the claim. It generally lays out what happened, why the recipient is considered legally responsible, and what the sender is seeking. Rather than being a court filing, a demand letter is usually a step in [negotiation](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/) — an invitation to resolve the matter without, or before, formal litigation.

Because it frames the claim, a demand letter can influence the tone and direction of everything that follows. A clear, well-supported letter may prompt a serious response, while a vague one may draw a low offer or none at all.

## What a demand letter typically contains

Although formats differ, many personal injury demand letters include a common set of elements:

- **A statement of facts** — a description of the incident and how the injury occurred.
- **A basis for liability** — an explanation of why the recipient is considered at fault, often framed in terms of negligence.
- **A summary of damages** — the losses claimed, which may include medical costs, lost income, and other harms.
- **A specific demand** — the remedy requested, commonly a dollar figure.
- **A response deadline** — a date by which the sender asks for a reply.

Supporting documentation is frequently attached or referenced, such as medical records or bills, though what is shared can depend on strategy and the stage of the dispute.

## How demand letters fit into negotiation

A demand letter often marks the beginning of [settlement](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/settlement) negotiations. The recipient — commonly an insurance adjuster — may accept, reject, or respond with a counteroffer. The figures then move back and forth as each side evaluates the claim. Many cases resolve at this stage, before a lawsuit is filed, which can save both sides the cost and time of litigation.

The letter can also carry practical weight later. It documents that a claim was communicated and describes the sender's position, which may become relevant if negotiations fail and the dispute proceeds to court.

## Time-limited and policy-limit demands

In the insurance context, demand letters sometimes take particular forms that carry added significance. A policy-limit demand asks an insurer to pay the full limits of the responsible party's coverage to resolve the claim. A time-limited demand sets a deadline by which the demand must be accepted before it is withdrawn.

These formats can matter beyond the immediate negotiation. In many jurisdictions, an insurer that unreasonably rejects a reasonable settlement demand within policy limits may expose itself to additional liability if a later judgment exceeds those limits, under legal theories addressing an insurer's duty to handle claims in good faith. Whether and how such exposure arises depends heavily on state law and on the specific facts, so these principles vary by jurisdiction.

Because of these potential consequences, demand letters of this kind are generally drafted with care, and the surrounding communications are often documented. Common features include:

- **A clear deadline** — the date by which a response is requested.
- **A defined demand** — the specific amount or limits sought.
- **Supporting material** — records that substantiate the injury and the losses claimed.

The legal effect of a time-limited or policy-limit demand is not uniform across the country. Some states have detailed statutes or case law governing how such demands operate and what an insurer must do in response, while others treat them more generally. As a result, the weight a particular demand carries depends on the governing jurisdiction.

## Limits, requirements, and variation by state

A demand letter is generally not required to bring an ordinary personal injury claim, but the surrounding rules vary by jurisdiction, and there are notable exceptions. Some states require a formal notice or a pre-suit demand in specific categories of cases. For example, claims against government entities often must follow strict notice-of-claim procedures with short deadlines set by state law, and certain consumer-protection or insurance statutes may require a demand before particular remedies become available.

The legal effect of a demand can also differ. In some jurisdictions, sending a proper demand and being met with an unreasonable refusal may affect the availability of certain remedies or cost-shifting under specific statutes. Because these requirements are state-specific, whether a demand is optional, advisable, or mandatory depends on the type of claim and the governing law. ## What happens after a demand

If the parties reach agreement, the claim is typically resolved through a settlement and release, and no lawsuit is filed. If they cannot agree, the sender may proceed to file a complaint, moving the dispute into formal litigation with its pleadings, discovery, and possible trial.

Either way, the demand letter often remains part of the record of how the claim developed, marking the point at which the injured party first stated, in writing, what resolution was sought. Because its content can be revisited later — whether in continued negotiation, in mediation, or in litigation — the accuracy and completeness of a demand are generally treated as significant, and how much weight it ultimately carries depends on the type of claim and the law of the governing jurisdiction.
