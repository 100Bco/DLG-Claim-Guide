## There is no single national deadline

A common assumption is that insurers have one fixed deadline to pay a claim. In fact, no single nationwide rule governs how long an insurer has to pay. Instead, payment timelines are set by each state, generally through what are known as prompt-payment or fair-claims-settlement rules. These laws establish how quickly an insurer must respond at each stage of a claim.

Because insurance is regulated at the state level, prompt-payment requirements are creatures of state statute and regulation. This means the answer to how long an insurer has to pay depends entirely on the state whose law applies to the policy and the loss. Figures from one state cannot be assumed to apply in another.

Many state frameworks draw on model standards from the [National Association of Insurance Commissioners](https://content.naic.org/), a body of state insurance regulators, but each state enacts and enforces its own version with its own deadlines.

## What the deadlines typically govern

Prompt-payment rules usually break the claim timeline into stages rather than imposing a single deadline. Common stages addressed by state rules include:

- **Acknowledgment** — how quickly the insurer must confirm it received the claim.
- **Investigation** — the period to investigate and request needed information.
- **Decision** — the time to accept or deny the claim after receiving the information needed to decide.
- **Payment** — how soon payment must follow once a claim is accepted.

Each stage may carry its own deadline, and the clocks often depend on when the insurer receives complete information. This staged structure is common, even though the specific time periods differ by state.

## State examples, clearly attributed

Because deadlines are state-specific, they are best understood through attributed examples rather than a single figure. Texas, for instance, addresses prompt payment through Chapter 542 of the [Texas Insurance Code](https://statutes.capitol.texas.gov/Docs/IN/htm/IN.542.htm), which sets deadlines for acknowledging a claim, requesting information, accepting or rejecting the claim after receiving requested items, and paying an accepted claim, along with consequences for late payment. Those particular deadlines are Texas's rules.

California addresses claim timelines through its fair claims settlement practices regulations, and the California Department of Insurance publishes consumer information about the periods that apply in that state. The specifics — such as how many days an insurer has to accept or deny after receiving proof of claim — differ between Texas, California, and other states. These figures illustrate the structure but apply only in the states that enact them; they should not be read as national standards.

## How rules and consequences vary by state

Beyond the deadlines themselves, states differ in what happens when an insurer misses them. Some states provide specific remedies for late payment, such as interest on the overdue amount or, in certain cases, additional penalties or attorney fees. Others rely more on regulatory enforcement by the state department of insurance. Whether a missed deadline creates a private remedy, and what that remedy is, depends on the jurisdiction.

The types of claims covered can also differ. Prompt-payment rules may apply differently to first-party property claims, liability claims, and health claims, and some categories are governed by separate statutes. Because of this variation, the deadline and the consequence for missing it depend on both the state and the type of claim.

## What can pause or extend the timeline

Prompt-payment deadlines generally do not run in a vacuum; many are tied to the insurer having the information it needs to decide. Understanding what can pause or extend the clock explains why a claim may take longer than the headline deadlines suggest.

Common factors that affect the timeline include:

- **Incomplete information** — many state deadlines to accept or deny begin only once the insurer receives the items it reasonably requested, so missing documentation can delay the clock.
- **Ongoing investigation** — where a claim raises questions of causation or possible fraud, statutes often allow additional time, sometimes with a requirement to update the policyholder.
- **Disputed or complex claims** — large or contested losses may involve experts and extended evaluation within the rules the state allows.
- **Requests for extensions** — some statutes permit an insurer to extend a deadline for defined reasons, often with notice.

These mechanisms are themselves defined by state law, so how much delay is permitted, and what the insurer must do to justify it, varies by jurisdiction. Importantly, the ability to extend a deadline is generally not unlimited; state rules typically require the insurer to act reasonably and to communicate, and unreasonable delay can itself raise fair-claims or bad-faith concerns in some states.

Because both the base deadlines and the grounds for pausing them are set by each state, the realistic timeline for a claim depends on the jurisdiction and the circumstances. The staged structure — acknowledge, investigate, decide, pay — remains common, but the clocks can move depending on what information is outstanding and what the governing state permits.

## What this means in practice

For any given claim, the applicable deadline comes from the state whose law governs the policy, not from a general rule. A policyholder tracking a claim's timeline generally looks to that state's prompt-payment statute or regulation, and to the state department of insurance for guidance on how the rules are applied and enforced.

Because these timelines and their remedies are set entirely at the state level, the honest answer to how long an insurer has to pay is that it depends on the jurisdiction. The common thread across states is a staged set of deadlines; the specific numbers, and what follows a missed deadline, are matters of each state's law.
