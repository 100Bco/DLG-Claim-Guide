## What diminished value means

Diminished value is the difference between what a vehicle was worth before an accident and what it is worth after being damaged and repaired. Even when a repair is done well, a vehicle with a documented accident history generally sells for less than an identical vehicle with a clean record. A diminished value claim seeks to recover that lost market value as a separate item from the cost of the repairs themselves.

The idea rests on a simple market reality: buyers and dealers discount vehicles that have been in a collision, because of concerns about hidden damage, resale, and reputation. Repairs restore function and appearance, but they do not erase the accident from the vehicle's history, so a gap in value can remain. That remaining gap is what a diminished value claim addresses.

Because whether this loss is compensable, and by whom, is governed by state insurance law and the terms of the applicable policy, diminished value is one of the more jurisdiction-dependent parts of an auto claim. The framework below describes how these claims commonly work rather than the rule of any single state.

## The main types of diminished value

Diminished value is usually divided into a few categories, and distinguishing them clarifies what a claim actually seeks.

- **Inherent diminished value** — the loss in value that remains simply because the vehicle now has an accident history, assuming repairs were done correctly. This is the most common basis for a claim.
- **Repair-related diminished value** — additional loss caused by repairs that were incomplete or of poor quality, leaving the vehicle in worse condition than proper repairs would have.
- **Immediate diminished value** — the difference in value right after the accident but before any repairs, a measure used more in some valuation contexts than others.

Most diminished value claims focus on inherent diminished value, since it captures the market's discount for a prior accident even after a proper repair. Which categories a state or policy recognizes can affect what is recoverable.

## First-party versus third-party claims

A central distinction in diminished value is who the claim is made against, and this is where state law matters most.

- **Third-party claims** — brought against the at-fault driver's liability insurer. Because that insurer is generally responsible for the full harm its insured caused, many states allow an injured vehicle owner to seek diminished value this way.
- **First-party claims** — brought against the vehicle owner's own insurer under their own collision coverage. Most states do not require an insurer to pay diminished value to its own insured, often because of policy language limiting the insurer's obligation to the cost of repair or replacement.

Georgia is the well-known exception on first-party claims. In *State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co. v. Mabry*, the Georgia Supreme Court held in 2001 that insurers were obligated to pay diminished value under their own policies in that state, a result most other states do not follow. Because the availability of a first-party claim turns on state law and policy terms, the same loss may be recoverable in one state and not in another.

## How diminished value is calculated

There is no single nationally mandated method for measuring diminished value, and different approaches can produce different numbers. In general, the loss is estimated by comparing the vehicle's value without an accident history to its value with one, taking into account the vehicle's make, model, age, mileage, condition, and the severity of the damage. Guidance from sources such as [J.D. Power](https://www.jdpower.com/cars/shopping-guides/how-to-calculate-diminished-value) describes how these estimates are commonly approached.

One widely referenced approach is the so-called 17c formula, which originated in the Mabry litigation as a settlement-administration shortcut. It applies a base loss figure capped at a percentage of the vehicle's value and then adjusts for damage severity and mileage. The 17c formula is frequently criticized as understating actual losses because of its caps, and it is a formula used by some insurers rather than a legal standard. Independent appraisals often reach different figures, which is one reason diminished value amounts are commonly disputed.

## How the rules vary by state

Diminished value is heavily state-specific. States differ in whether they allow first-party claims at all, in how they treat third-party claims, and in the deadlines and proof required. They also differ in practical respects, such as whether an insurer will consider an independent appraisal and how disputes over the amount are resolved, sometimes through a policy's appraisal provision. Consumer information from insurance regulators, including the [National Association of Insurance Commissioners](https://content.naic.org/) and state insurance departments, reflects that these rules are set at the state level.

The type of coverage involved also shapes the claim. A third-party claim depends on establishing the other driver's fault and the resulting loss, while a first-party claim depends on the policy's language and the state's treatment of such claims. Because of this variation, the general framework here should be confirmed against the law of the relevant state and the terms of the policy in question.

## What this means in practice

In practical terms, a diminished value claim is usually a separate step from getting a vehicle repaired, and it often requires evidence — such as an independent appraisal — to support the amount claimed. Whether the claim is even available depends first on the state and on whether it is brought against the at-fault driver's insurer or one's own. As the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/) and state regulators note, the details of auto claims, including how value is measured, are governed by state rules and policy terms.

Because the availability of a diminished value claim, the method of calculating the loss, and the way disputes are resolved all depend on the jurisdiction and the applicable policy, how any particular diminished value claim unfolds turns on those specifics. The overview here explains how these claims work rather than predicting the outcome of an individual claim.
