## What an insurance claim is

An insurance claim is a formal request by a policyholder asking an insurer to provide the coverage promised under an [insurance](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/insurance) policy. Insurance is fundamentally a contract: in exchange for premiums, the insurer agrees to pay for certain covered losses, subject to the policy's terms, conditions, and exclusions. A claim is how that promise is invoked after a loss occurs.

Because the policy is a contract, its language largely controls what is covered and what is required to make a claim. Terms such as the covered perils, the exclusions, the deductible, and the limits of coverage all shape how a claim is evaluated. Reading a claim, then, generally starts with reading the policy that governs it.

Insurance is also heavily regulated at the state level. Each state has a department of insurance that oversees insurers, and state laws and regulations set baseline requirements for how claims are handled. This combination of private contract and public regulation is why claim handling can differ from one policy and one state to the next.

## The typical steps in a claim

While the details vary by policy and jurisdiction, most property and general claims move through a recognizable sequence:

- **First notice of loss** — the policyholder reports the loss to the insurer, which opens the claim and assigns a file.
- **Investigation** — the insurer gathers facts, which may include reviewing the policy, inspecting the damage, and requesting documents.
- **Adjustment** — an insurance adjuster evaluates the loss, estimates the covered amount, and applies the policy terms, including any deductible.
- **Decision** — the insurer accepts the claim and pays, makes a partial payment, or denies the claim with a stated reason.
- **Payment or resolution** — for accepted claims, the insurer issues payment according to the policy and any applicable timelines.

Each step generally has associated obligations for both sides, such as the policyholder's duty to cooperate and the insurer's duty to investigate reasonably.

## Documentation and the policyholder's role

Claims generally depend on documentation. Insurers typically ask for information establishing that a covered loss occurred and the amount of that loss. Common items include photographs, receipts, repair estimates, and, for many property claims, a formal proof of loss — a signed statement detailing the claim.

Policies usually impose duties on the policyholder after a loss, such as promptly reporting the claim, taking reasonable steps to prevent further damage (often called mitigation), and cooperating with the investigation. Failing to meet these duties can, in some circumstances, affect a claim. Because these obligations are set by the policy and interpreted under state law, their exact scope depends on the contract and the jurisdiction.

## How timelines and rules vary by state

Although the general process is similar nationwide, the specific rules differ significantly by state. States regulate claim handling through statutes and regulations, often modeled in part on standards from the National Association of Insurance Commissioners, a body of state regulators whose model laws many states have adopted in some form. Even so, each state adapts and enforces its own version.

State rules commonly address how quickly an insurer must acknowledge a claim, begin an investigation, and accept or deny it, as well as standards for fair claim handling. These deadlines and standards vary widely, so a timeline that applies in one state should not be assumed to apply in another. State departments of insurance, such as the [California Department of Insurance](https://www.insurance.ca.gov/), publish consumer information explaining the rules in their jurisdiction and generally accept complaints about claim handling.

## Common points of dispute

Even a straightforward claim can involve disagreement, and understanding where disputes tend to arise clarifies how the process can stall. Most disagreements fall into a few recurring categories.

- **Coverage disputes** — whether the loss is covered at all, often turning on a policy exclusion or the definition of a covered peril.
- **Causation disputes** — what actually caused the loss, which can matter when one cause is covered and another is not.
- **Valuation disputes** — how much the covered loss is worth, including questions of depreciation and repair scope.
- **Documentation gaps** — disagreements about whether the policyholder has provided enough proof of the loss and its value.

The distinction between coverage disputes and valuation disputes is particularly important, because they often follow different resolution paths. Many property policies provide an appraisal process for disagreements about the amount of a loss, while questions about whether coverage exists are generally resolved through other means, such as an internal appeal or the courts.

How these disputes are handled is shaped by both the policy language and state law. States set standards for reasonable investigation and fair claim handling, and a dispute that seems purely factual may also raise questions about whether the insurer met those standards. Because the governing rules differ by jurisdiction, the way a particular dispute is resolved depends on the state and the policy. Recognizing which type of dispute is at issue is generally the first step toward identifying the appropriate response.

## What happens after a decision

Once an insurer issues a decision, several paths are possible. If the claim is accepted, payment generally follows under the policy and any state prompt-payment rules. If the claim is partially paid or denied, the policyholder may have options, including submitting additional information, requesting an internal appeal, invoking a policy's appraisal process for disputes about the amount of loss, filing a complaint with the state insurance regulator, or, in some cases, pursuing the matter in court.

Because the process rests on both the policy contract and state regulation, the practical experience of a claim depends on the specific coverage and the jurisdiction. Understanding the policy's terms and the applicable state rules is generally the foundation for understanding how any particular claim will be handled.
