When a person purchases an insurance policy, they are entering into a contract. Implicit in every insurance contract is a legal concept known as the "implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing." This means the insurance company is legally obligated to treat the policyholder fairly and process claims reasonably.

A bad faith claim occurs when an insurer violates this duty. It is important to distinguish between a simple contract dispute—where an insurer legitimately believes a claim is not covered under the policy—and bad faith, which involves unreasonable or deceptive conduct.

### Examples of Bad Faith Practices

While laws vary by state, certain practices are commonly recognized as indicators of bad faith:

*   **Unreasonable delays:** Failing to acknowledge receipt of a claim promptly, or intentionally dragging out the investigation process without cause.
*   **Inadequate investigation:** Denying a claim without conducting a thorough, objective review of the facts and evidence.
*   **Deceptive practices:** Misrepresenting policy language, failing to disclose available coverage, or attempting to settle a claim for substantially less than a reasonable person would believe they are entitled to.
*   **Unjustified denial:** Denying a valid claim without providing a clear, written explanation based on the specific language of the policy.

### First-Party vs. Third-Party Bad Faith

Bad faith claims generally fall into two categories. **First-party bad faith** involves an insurer's conduct toward its own policyholder (for example, a homeowner filing a claim after a fire). **Third-party bad faith** involves an insurer's failure to reasonably defend or settle a claim brought against its policyholder by someone else (for example, failing to settle a liability claim within policy limits, exposing the policyholder to a massive personal judgment).

If a court determines an insurer acted in bad faith, the policyholder may be entitled to recover not only the original value of the claim but also extra-contractual damages, which can include emotional distress and, in some cases, punitive damages designed to punish the insurer's conduct.
