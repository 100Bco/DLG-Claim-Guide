## What subrogation means

Subrogation is a legal principle that allows one party who has paid a debt or loss to take over another's right to recover from the party truly responsible. In the [insurance](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/insurance) context, [subrogation](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/subrogation) generally means that when an insurer pays its policyholder for a loss caused by someone else, the insurer may then pursue that at-fault party to recover what it paid. The insurer effectively "steps into the shoes" of the policyholder for that purpose.

The principle serves two related goals. It prevents a double recovery — the policyholder generally should not collect the full loss twice, once from the insurer and again from the wrongdoer. And it places the ultimate cost of the loss on the party who caused it, rather than leaving it with the insurer or spreading it across other policyholders.

Because subrogation arises from both the policy contract and general legal principles, its scope and mechanics are shaped by state law and vary by jurisdiction.

## How subrogation typically works

Subrogation usually unfolds after an insurer has paid a claim. The general sequence includes:

- **The insurer pays the claim** — the policyholder is compensated for the covered loss.
- **The insurer identifies a responsible party** — a person or entity whose conduct caused the loss.
- **The insurer pursues recovery** — it seeks reimbursement from that party or the party's insurer, exercising the rights the policyholder would have had.
- **Recovered funds are applied** — the insurer keeps recovery up to what it paid, with rules governing any remainder.

Policies commonly include a subrogation clause requiring the policyholder to cooperate and not to undermine the insurer's recovery rights, for example by releasing the at-fault party without consent.

## Contractual, equitable, and statutory subrogation

Subrogation can arise in more than one way, and the type affects how it operates. Contractual subrogation comes from language in the policy itself. Equitable subrogation arises from general principles of fairness applied by courts even without specific policy language. Statutory subrogation is created or regulated by a state statute for particular contexts.

These sources can overlap, and states treat them differently. The distinctions can matter for questions such as how far the insurer's rights extend, what defenses a policyholder or third party may raise, and how competing claims to a limited recovery are prioritized.

## How subrogation affects a policyholder — and varies by state

Subrogation can directly affect a policyholder's own recovery, and this is where state variation becomes especially important. A frequent issue is the "made whole" doctrine, under which many states hold that an insurer generally may not recover through subrogation until the policyholder has been fully compensated for the loss. States apply this doctrine differently, and in some it can be modified by policy language, while in others it is more protective of the policyholder.

Related rules address the "common fund" doctrine, under which an insurer that benefits from a recovery the policyholder obtained may be required to share in the attorney fees and costs of obtaining it. Whether and how these doctrines apply depends on the state. Because subrogation sits at the intersection of the policy and state law, its effect on any recovery depends on the jurisdiction; for a sense of how these dealings unfold in one state, see [how one state approaches dealings with insurers](https://danglawgroup.com/guide-to-negotiating-with-insurance-companies-in-texas/).

## Waivers and limits on subrogation

Subrogation rights are not unlimited, and several well-recognized rules restrict them. Understanding these limits explains why an insurer cannot always recover from every party connected to a loss.

- **Waiver of subrogation** — parties sometimes agree in advance, often in a contract or lease, that an insurer will waive its subrogation rights against the other party. Many policies permit such waivers if made before a loss, which shifts the risk as the parties intended.
- **The anti-subrogation rule** — in many states, an insurer generally may not pursue subrogation against its own insured, because doing so would effectively have the insurer recover from the very party it agreed to protect.
- **The made-whole limitation** — as noted, many states restrict an insurer's recovery until the policyholder has been fully compensated.

These limits interact with contracts and with state law in ways that vary by jurisdiction. Waiver-of-subrogation clauses are common in construction and commercial leasing, where the parties allocate risk to insurance deliberately. The anti-subrogation rule protects policyholders from being sued by their own insurer, but its precise scope — including how it treats additional insureds — differs by state.

Because subrogation sits at the intersection of the policy, other contracts, and state law, whether an insurer can recover in a given situation depends on all three. A waiver in a lease, the identity of the target party, and the governing state's version of the made-whole and anti-subrogation rules can each determine the outcome. As with other insurance doctrines, the specifics depend on the jurisdiction and the documents involved.

## Where subrogation commonly appears

Subrogation surfaces across many types of insurance. In property claims, an insurer that pays for damage may pursue a manufacturer or contractor whose defective product or work caused it. In auto claims, an insurer that pays its policyholder may seek recovery from an at-fault driver or that driver's insurer. In health coverage, an insurer or plan that paid accident-related medical bills may assert subrogation or reimbursement against a later personal injury recovery, a concept closely related to medical liens.

Because subrogation can reduce what an injured or damaged party ultimately keeps, and because the governing doctrines differ by state, its practical impact depends on the type of claim, the policy language, and the law of the jurisdiction involved.
