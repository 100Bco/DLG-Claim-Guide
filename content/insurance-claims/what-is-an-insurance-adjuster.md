## What an adjuster does

An insurance adjuster is the person responsible for investigating a claim and estimating the amount an insurer owes under an [insurance](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/insurance) policy. When a claim is filed, the adjuster generally gathers the facts, inspects the loss, reviews the policy, and applies its terms to reach a valuation. The adjuster's assessment often forms the basis for the insurer's decision to pay, partially pay, or deny.

The role combines investigation and evaluation. On the investigation side, an adjuster may examine damaged property, interview the policyholder and witnesses, review documents, and consult experts. On the evaluation side, the adjuster interprets the policy — its covered perils, exclusions, limits, and deductible — and estimates the covered loss.

Because the adjuster's work drives the claim outcome, how adjusters are trained, licensed, and expected to behave is regulated by each state, which is why standards can differ by jurisdiction.

## The main types of adjusters

Not all adjusters serve the same interests. The three common types differ in who employs them and whom they represent:

- **Staff (company) adjuster** — an employee of the insurer who handles claims on the insurer's behalf.
- **Independent adjuster** — a contractor hired by an insurer to handle claims, often during high-volume periods, still working for the insurer.
- **Public adjuster** — an adjuster retained by the policyholder to represent the policyholder's interests in preparing and negotiating a claim, typically for a fee.

Staff and independent adjusters both act for the insurer, while a public adjuster works for the policyholder. This distinction matters because it determines whose interests the adjuster is advancing during the claim.

## Licensing and conduct standards

Most states regulate adjusters through licensing requirements, and many also regulate public adjusters separately, given that they charge policyholders. Licensing generally aims to ensure that adjusters meet baseline qualifications and follow conduct standards. State insurance regulators, such as the [Texas Department of Insurance](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/), commonly oversee adjuster licensing and can address complaints about adjuster conduct.

Conduct standards often draw on fair claim-handling rules, many of which trace to model standards from the National Association of Insurance Commissioners that states have adopted in varying forms. These standards may address timeliness, communication, and reasonable investigation. Public adjusters are frequently subject to additional rules, such as caps on their fees or restrictions on solicitation, which differ by state.

## How rules vary by state

Adjuster regulation is state-specific, and the details differ considerably. States vary in whether and how they license staff, independent, and public adjusters, what qualifications and examinations they require, how they cap or regulate public adjuster fees, and what conduct standards apply. Some states impose relatively detailed requirements, while others regulate more lightly, and a few treat certain adjuster categories differently.

Because these rules are set and enforced by each state's department of insurance, the licensing status and obligations of an adjuster depend on the jurisdiction. A public adjuster's permitted fee or a staff adjuster's conduct duties in one state should not be assumed to match another.

## How adjusters reach a valuation

An adjuster's central task is translating a loss into a dollar figure the policy will pay, and understanding how that valuation is built clarifies why estimates can differ. The process generally combines a physical or documentary assessment with the policy's valuation rules.

Common elements of an adjuster's valuation include:

- **Scope of loss** — an itemized description of what was damaged and what repair or replacement each item requires.
- **Pricing** — unit costs for materials and labor, often generated with standardized estimating software.
- **Valuation method** — applying the policy's approach, such as actual cash value or replacement cost, which determines whether depreciation is subtracted.
- **Deductible and limits** — reducing the covered amount by the deductible and capping it at the policy limit.

Because different adjusters may assess the scope, pricing, or depreciation differently, two estimates of the same loss can vary. This is a frequent source of disagreement between a policyholder and an insurer, and it is precisely the kind of dispute the appraisal process is designed to resolve when it concerns the amount rather than coverage.

Insurers also set aside a reserve — an internal estimate of what a claim may ultimately cost — which is part of claim management but distinct from the amount owed to the policyholder. How valuations are performed and disclosed is influenced by state fair-claims standards, which vary by jurisdiction. Because the valuation drives the payment, documentation supporting the scope and cost of a loss generally plays a central role, and the governing state's rules shape how disputes over that valuation are handled.

## Working with an adjuster during a claim

In a typical claim, the policyholder interacts with the adjuster throughout the process — reporting the loss, providing documentation, allowing inspection, and discussing the estimate. Because the adjuster's valuation often determines the payment, documentation supporting the claim generally plays a central role, including photographs, receipts, and estimates.

Disagreements with an adjuster's valuation are common and do not necessarily end a claim. Depending on the policy and state, a dispute about the amount of loss may be resolved through the policy's appraisal process, while broader disputes may lead to an internal appeal, a regulator complaint, or litigation. Because the adjuster operates within both the policy contract and state regulation, the significance of any adjuster's decision depends on the governing jurisdiction and the terms of the policy.
