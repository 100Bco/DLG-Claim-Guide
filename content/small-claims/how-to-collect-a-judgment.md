## A judgment is not the same as payment

A common surprise for those who win a case is that a judgment — the court's official ruling that one party owes another a sum of money — does not itself transfer any money. The court determines who owes what, but it generally does not act as a collection agency. If the losing party, often called the judgment debtor, pays voluntarily, the matter ends. If not, the prevailing party, the judgment creditor, generally has to take additional steps to enforce the judgment.

This gap between winning and collecting is significant. Enforcement is a separate phase with its own procedures, and success can depend on whether the debtor has income or assets that the law allows to be reached. A judgment against a person with few reachable resources may be difficult to collect, even though it is legally valid.

Because enforcement procedures are set by each state, the available tools and the steps to use them differ by jurisdiction. State court self-help resources generally explain the collection options for their courts.

## Common collection tools

States authorize several methods to enforce a money judgment. The most common tools reach a debtor's income or property:

- **Wage garnishment** — a court order directing a portion of the debtor's earnings to be withheld and paid toward the judgment.
- **Bank levy** — an order allowing funds in the debtor's bank account to be seized to satisfy the judgment.
- **Property lien** — a claim recorded against the debtor's real estate, which can allow the debt to be paid when the property is sold or refinanced.
- **Personal property execution** — seizure and sale of certain non-exempt personal property in some states.

The availability and mechanics of tools such as [garnishment](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/garnishment) are defined by state law, and using them generally requires following specific court procedures.

## Finding the debtor's assets

Because collection depends on reaching income or property, identifying what a debtor has is often a key step. Many states provide a procedure, sometimes called a debtor's examination or examination of the judgment debtor, that allows the creditor to require the debtor to answer questions under oath about income, employment, bank accounts, and property.

This process exists because a creditor generally cannot garnish wages or levy an account without knowing where the debtor works or banks. The rules for compelling a debtor to disclose this information, and the consequences of failing to appear, are set by each state, so the availability and force of this tool vary by jurisdiction.

## Exemptions and limits vary by state

A central feature of collection law is that not all income and property can be taken. Both federal and state law protect certain assets from collection through what are called exemptions. Federal law limits how much of a person's wages may be garnished, and states set their own additional exemptions for wages, bank funds, a home (often called a homestead exemption), vehicles, and other property. Some public benefits are generally protected from garnishment.

These exemptions differ substantially by state, and they can significantly affect what a creditor can actually collect. Court self-help centers, such as the [California Courts Self-Help Center](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/), explain the exemptions and collection procedures in their jurisdictions. Because the protections are state-specific, the practical collectability of a judgment depends on the jurisdiction and the debtor's circumstances.

## Voluntary payment and payment arrangements

Before turning to enforcement tools, collection often begins with the simplest path: asking the judgment debtor to pay. Many debtors pay voluntarily once a judgment is entered, and a straightforward written request identifying the judgment and the amount owed is generally the starting point. Enforcement measures such as garnishment typically become relevant only when voluntary payment does not occur.

Several arrangements can arise at this stage:

- **Lump-sum payment** — the debtor pays the full amount, resolving the judgment.
- **Payment plan** — the parties agree, or a court in some states orders, payment in installments over time.
- **Partial payment** — payments that reduce the balance, with the remainder still enforceable.

When a judgment is paid in full, the creditor is generally expected to acknowledge it, often by filing a document with the court — commonly called a satisfaction of judgment — confirming the debt is resolved. This step matters because an unsatisfied judgment can remain on record and may affect the debtor, so documenting payment protects both sides.

Because the procedures for payment plans, court-ordered installments, and recording satisfaction are set by each state, they vary by jurisdiction. State court self-help centers explain how their courts handle voluntary payment and the forms for acknowledging it. Pursuing voluntary payment first is generally emphasized because it can resolve a judgment without the time and expense of formal enforcement, which the state's rules make available if payment is not forthcoming.

## What typically happens over time

Judgments generally remain enforceable for a period set by state law, often a number of years, and many states allow a judgment to be renewed before it expires. In some states, unpaid judgments accrue interest at a statutory rate, increasing the amount owed over time. These features mean a judgment that cannot be collected immediately may still be enforceable later if the debtor's situation changes.

Because every step of enforcement — the tools, the exemptions, the duration, and any interest — is governed by state law, collecting a judgment is ultimately a state-specific process. The judgment establishes the debt; the state's enforcement rules determine how, and how much of it, can actually be recovered.
