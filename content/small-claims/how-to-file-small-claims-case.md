## Identifying the right court

Filing a small claims case generally begins with determining where the case belongs. Two questions typically matter: whether the dispute is within the small claims dollar limit, and which specific court and location are proper. The correct location, often called venue, is usually tied to where the defendant lives or does business, or where the events giving rise to the dispute occurred.

Choosing the wrong court can delay or complicate a case, so state judiciaries generally provide guidance on venue. Because small claims cases may be heard in differently named courts depending on the state — small claims court, justice court, magistrate court, or a division of a larger trial court — identifying the right one is a state-specific step.

The dollar limit also affects filing. A claim above the small claims cap generally cannot proceed in small claims court, and in some states a claimant may choose to reduce a claim to fit within the limit, giving up the excess. These choices are governed by state law.

## Completing and submitting the claim

The core of filing is a claim form that identifies the parties and describes the dispute. Although forms differ by state, they generally call for a common set of information:

- **The parties** — the correct legal names and addresses of the claimant and the defendant.
- **The amount claimed** — the sum sought, within the court's limit.
- **The basis for the claim** — a short description of what happened and why the defendant allegedly owes the money.
- **Supporting details** — dates, and sometimes references to documents such as contracts or receipts.

Naming the defendant accurately is often emphasized, because a judgment against a misnamed party can be difficult to enforce. After completing the form, the claimant files it with the court and pays a filing fee, which varies by jurisdiction and sometimes by the amount of the claim.

## Serving the defendant

Once a case is filed, the defendant generally must be formally notified through service of process — the delivery of official notice of the lawsuit. Proper service is important because a court generally cannot enter a valid judgment against a defendant who was not properly served. States specify who may serve the papers, how service may be accomplished, and how it is proven to the court.

Court self-help resources, such as those published by the [Utah State Courts](https://www.utcourts.gov/), explain the service methods available in their jurisdiction, which may include personal delivery, certified mail, or service by a sheriff or process server. Because service rules are state-specific and strict, following the correct method for the jurisdiction is generally important to keeping a case on track.

## How rules and fees vary by state

The mechanics of filing are highly state- and county-specific. States differ in the forms required, the filing fees, the deadlines for filing after a dispute arises, the permitted methods of service, and even whether certain parties, such as businesses or those who have filed many claims, face additional limits. Some states cap how many small claims a person may file in a year.

Because of this variation, the correct forms and steps depend on the jurisdiction. State court self-help centers, such as the [California Courts Self-Help Center](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/), publish the specific instructions, forms, and fee schedules for their courts, each reflecting that state's rules rather than a national procedure.

## Fees, fee waivers, and preparation

Filing a small claims case generally involves a fee, and the amount varies by state and sometimes by the size of the claim. Because access to the courts is a public concern, most states provide a way for people who cannot afford the fee to request a waiver, often called a fee waiver or proceeding in forma pauperis, based on income or receipt of public benefits.

Common cost-related considerations include:

- **Filing fees** — paid when the claim is submitted, set by the jurisdiction and sometimes tiered by claim amount.
- **Service fees** — separate costs for serving the defendant, which depend on the method used.
- **Fee waivers** — available in many states for those who qualify, generally requiring a short application.

Beyond fees, preparation is often emphasized in court self-help materials. Because the hearing is short, assembling the relevant documents — contracts, receipts, photographs, and communications — and organizing them before filing can help present the claim clearly later. Confirming the defendant's correct legal name and current address is also important, both for proper service and for enforcing any judgment.

Because the fees, waiver standards, and preparation expectations are set by each state and local court, they vary by jurisdiction. State court self-help centers publish the current fee schedules and waiver forms for their courts, each reflecting that state's rules. Understanding these costs at the outset is generally part of deciding whether and how to bring a small claims case.

## What happens after filing

After the claim is filed and the defendant is served, the court generally sets a hearing date and notifies the parties. In many states, the defendant may respond, and in some may file a claim of their own against the claimant, known as a counterclaim, if they believe the claimant owes them money arising from the same dispute.

From there, the case proceeds toward the hearing, where both sides present their evidence to a judge. Because each of these steps — filing, service, response, and hearing — is governed by state and local rules, the path of a small claims case depends on the jurisdiction where it is filed.
