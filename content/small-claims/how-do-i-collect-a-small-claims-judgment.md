A common misconception about the small claims process is that the court will collect the money on the winner's behalf. In reality, the court simply issues a judgment—a legal declaration that the defendant (the debtor) owes the plaintiff (the creditor) a specific amount of money.

If the debtor does not voluntarily write a check after the judgment is entered, the burden falls entirely on the creditor to enforce the order. This process requires returning to court to request specific enforcement mechanisms.

### Locating the Debtor's Assets

Before a creditor can seize money or property, they must know where it is. If the creditor does not know where the debtor banks or works, they can often request a post-judgment discovery process. This may involve forcing the debtor back to court for a debtor's examination—a hearing under oath where they must answer questions about their finances, bank accounts, and employment.

### Methods of Collection

Once the creditor has identified the debtor's assets, they can apply for court orders to seize them. The most common tools include:

*   **Bank Levies:** An order directing the debtor's bank to freeze the account and turn over funds to satisfy the judgment. This is often the most effective method if the creditor knows where the debtor banks.
*   **Wage Garnishment:** An order directing the debtor's employer to withhold a portion of the debtor's paycheck each pay period and send it to the creditor. Federal and state laws strictly limit the percentage of a paycheck that can be garnished to ensure the debtor can still afford basic living expenses.
*   **Property Liens:** Filing a lien against the debtor's real estate. This does not result in immediate cash, but it ensures that if the debtor attempts to sell or refinance the property, the judgment must be paid off from the proceeds.

Enforcing a judgment requires paying additional filing fees (which can often be added to the judgment total) and strictly following procedural rules. Judgments are also subject to statutes of limitations, meaning they will eventually expire if they are not collected or renewed within a certain number of years.
