## Evidence in a simplified setting

Evidence is the information a party presents to prove the facts of a dispute. In formal civil trials, detailed rules of [evidence](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/evidence) govern what a judge or jury may consider. Small claims court generally takes a more relaxed approach, because the system is designed for people representing themselves and for quick resolution.

This does not mean anything goes. The judge still decides what is relevant and how much weight to give each item, and evidence generally needs to relate to the dispute. But the technical objections and exclusions common in formal trials are often applied more loosely, allowing parties to present their case in a practical way.

Because the rules of evidence and how strictly they apply are set by each state, the degree of formality can differ by jurisdiction. Some states expressly relax the evidence rules for small claims, while others apply them more conventionally.

## Common types of evidence

Small claims cases generally turn on a mix of documents and testimony. Frequently used forms of evidence include:

- **Documents** — contracts, invoices, receipts, estimates, leases, and written communications such as emails or texts.
- **Photographs and video** — images of property damage, conditions, or other physical facts.
- **Witness testimony** — statements from people with firsthand knowledge of the facts.
- **Records** — account statements, logs, or other business records relevant to the claim.

Presenting organized copies, and bringing enough copies for the court and the other side where required, is commonly recommended in court self-help materials. Because a hearing is short, evidence that is clear and easy to follow generally helps the judge understand the dispute.

## Relevance, authenticity, and hearsay

Even in a relaxed setting, a few evidentiary concepts still tend to matter. Relevance asks whether the evidence relates to a fact in dispute. Authenticity asks whether an item is what it claims to be — for example, that a document is genuine. Hearsay, an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of what it asserts, is restricted in formal trials but is often treated more permissively in small claims court.

How these concepts apply varies by state. Some states allow judges broad discretion to consider evidence that would face objections in a formal trial, while others adhere more closely to standard rules. The judge generally decides how much weight to give each item, so even admitted evidence may be more or less persuasive depending on its reliability.

## How evidence rules vary by state

The treatment of evidence is one of the areas where small claims practice differs most by jurisdiction. States vary in whether the formal rules of evidence apply, how they handle hearsay and documentary evidence, whether written witness statements are accepted in place of live testimony, and what must be exchanged or brought to the hearing. Some states permit sworn written declarations, while others expect witnesses to appear in person.

State court self-help centers, such as the [California Courts Self-Help Center](https://selfhelp.courts.ca.gov/), explain what evidence their courts accept and how to prepare it. Because these rules are state-specific, the admissibility and handling of evidence depend on the jurisdiction, and practices in one state should not be assumed to apply in another.

## Digital evidence and physical exhibits

Modern disputes increasingly involve digital evidence, and small claims courts generally accept it, though presenting it well can take some planning. Text messages, emails, online reviews, account screenshots, and photographs stored on a phone are common in everyday disputes, but a hearing generally requires them in a form the judge can review.

Practical considerations for such evidence include:

- **Printed copies** — many courts prefer printed versions of texts, emails, or screenshots rather than a phone screen, and often require copies for the court and the other side.
- **Context and dating** — showing who sent a message and when can matter for how the judge weighs it.
- **Physical exhibits** — actual items, or clear photographs of them, can help illustrate damage or a defect.

Authenticity remains relevant even in an informal setting: the judge generally considers whether an item is what it purports to be. A party presenting a screenshot or document may be asked to explain where it came from. While small claims courts often apply these concepts loosely, being able to account for the source of evidence tends to make it more persuasive.

Because the accepted formats and any requirements for copies are set by each state and local court, they vary by jurisdiction. State court self-help centers explain how their courts prefer evidence to be submitted. The general principle is that evidence — digital or physical — is most useful when it is clear, properly identified, and easy for the judge to examine within the short span of a hearing.

## Preparing evidence for a hearing

Because the hearing is brief and the judge must grasp the dispute quickly, preparation generally focuses on clarity and organization. Assembling documents in a logical order, tying each piece of evidence to a point in the dispute, and being ready to explain what each item shows are commonly emphasized in self-help guidance.

Ultimately, the persuasive value of evidence depends on how clearly it establishes the facts and how the judge weighs it under the applicable state rules. Because both admissibility and weight are governed by the law of the jurisdiction, the role evidence plays in any small claims case depends on the state where it is heard.
