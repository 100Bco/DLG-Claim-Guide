## What service of process is

Service of process is the formal procedure by which a party to a lawsuit delivers the required legal documents to the other party, giving official notice that a case has begun. In a small claims case, this generally means delivering the claim and the notice of hearing to the defendant. The term "process" refers to the court papers that compel a party's involvement in the case.

The requirement serves a basic principle of fairness embodied in the concept of due process: a person generally cannot be bound by a court's decision without first being notified of the case and given an opportunity to be heard. [Service of process](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/service_of_process) is how the legal system delivers that notice in a formal, verifiable way.

Because the rules governing service are set by each state, the acceptable methods, who may serve, and how service is documented differ by jurisdiction. Proper service is generally treated as a prerequisite to the court proceeding against a defendant.

## Why proper service matters

Service is not a mere formality; it connects to the court's authority over the defendant. A court generally needs personal jurisdiction — power over the parties — to enter a valid judgment, and proper service is a key part of establishing that a defendant was brought into the case correctly. If service is defective, a resulting judgment may be vulnerable to challenge.

The consequences of improper service can be significant. A defendant who was not properly served may be able to have a default judgment — a judgment entered when a party does not respond — set aside. This is why courts and self-help resources emphasize following the exact method the state allows and proving that service was completed.

## Common methods of service

States authorize different methods of service, and small claims courts often provide specific options suited to their simplified process. Common methods include:

- **Personal service** — handing the documents directly to the defendant, often considered the most reliable method.
- **Substituted service** — leaving the documents with another suitable person at the defendant's home or workplace, then following up as the state requires.
- **Service by mail** — sending the documents by certified or registered mail, allowed for small claims in some states.
- **Service by a sheriff or process server** — using an official or a neutral adult, depending on the jurisdiction's rules.

Most states require the person who bears the burden of the claim to arrange service through a neutral party rather than doing it personally. Court self-help resources, such as those published by the [Utah State Courts](https://www.utcourts.gov/), describe the methods available and any deadlines for completing service.

## How service rules vary by state

Service of process is heavily state-specific, and the details differ in important ways. States vary in which methods are permitted for small claims, who may perform service, how far in advance of the hearing service must be completed, and how service is proven — often through a document called a proof of service or return of service. Some states restrict service by mail or require additional steps if the defendant cannot be located.

Because these rules are strict and jurisdiction-specific, following the correct procedure for the state is generally essential. State court self-help centers, such as the California Courts Self-Help Center, publish the accepted methods and forms for their courts. As with other aspects of small claims practice, service rules in one state should not be assumed to apply elsewhere.

## Proof of service and deadlines

Completing service is generally only part of the requirement; the court usually also needs proof that service occurred. This proof commonly takes the form of a document — often called a proof of service or return of service — filed with the court, describing who was served, when, where, and by what method. The person who performed the service typically signs it.

This documentation matters because the court relies on it to confirm the defendant received proper notice before proceeding. Without adequate proof of service, a court may be unable to move forward or to enter a valid judgment, even if the defendant was in fact notified.

Timing is also generally regulated:

- **Advance service** — many states require service to be completed a minimum number of days before the hearing, so the defendant has time to prepare.
- **Filing the proof** — the proof of service often must be filed by a set point before the hearing.
- **Re-service** — if service was not completed in time or was defective, the court may continue the hearing to allow another attempt.

Because these deadlines and proof requirements are set by each state and local court, they vary by jurisdiction, and missing them can delay a case. State court self-help centers publish the applicable deadlines and the proof-of-service forms for their courts. As with the methods themselves, the timing and documentation of service are governed by the law of the jurisdiction where the case is filed.

## What happens after service

Once the defendant has been properly served, the case generally proceeds toward the hearing. The claimant typically files proof that service was completed, which allows the court to confirm the defendant received notice. If service was accomplished correctly and the defendant does not appear, the court may proceed and, in many states, enter a default judgment for the claimant.

If service could not be completed — for example, because the defendant could not be found — the court generally cannot proceed until proper notice is achieved, and the claimant may need to attempt an alternative method the state allows. Because both the methods and the consequences of service are set by state law, how service affects a case depends on the jurisdiction in which it is filed.
