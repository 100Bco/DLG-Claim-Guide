## Duration depends on the type of benefit

There is no single answer to how long workers' compensation benefits last, because different benefits follow different rules. Workers' compensation generally provides several distinct types of benefits, and each has its own timeline. The most important distinction is between medical benefits and wage-replacement (indemnity) benefits.

Medical benefits cover treatment for the work injury. In many states, they may continue for as long as care remains reasonable, necessary, and related to the injury — potentially for a long time in serious cases — though some states place limits on certain types of treatment. Wage-replacement benefits, by contrast, are more commonly subject to defined caps.

Because these durations are set by each state's statute, the length of benefits can differ substantially from one jurisdiction to another, and figures from one state should not be assumed to apply elsewhere.

## Wage-replacement benefits and their limits

Wage-replacement benefits partially make up for income lost because of a work injury. They are usually divided into categories, each with its own duration rules:

- **Temporary total disability** — paid while a worker cannot work at all during recovery; often subject to a cap on the number of weeks.
- **Temporary partial disability** — paid when a worker can do some work at reduced earnings during recovery.
- **Permanent partial disability** — paid when an injury leaves lasting but partial impairment, frequently tied to an impairment rating and a benefit schedule.
- **Permanent total disability** — paid when an injury permanently prevents a return to gainful work; may last longer, sometimes for life in some states.

For example, [California's Labor Code](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=LAB&sectionNum=4656) generally limits most temporary disability payments to 104 weeks within a defined period — a figure specific to California, not a nationwide standard. Other states set different caps, and some measure duration differently.

## What can end or extend benefits

Several developments can affect how long benefits continue. Reaching maximum medical improvement — the point at which a condition stabilizes — often ends temporary disability benefits and shifts the focus to any permanent impairment. A return to work, either full or partial, can end or reduce wage-replacement benefits. A settlement may resolve future benefits in a lump sum, closing out some or all of the claim depending on its terms.

Benefits can also end if the worker misses required medical evaluations or fails to follow the state's procedures, or if the insurer successfully disputes ongoing entitlement. Conversely, benefits may be extended or reopened in some states if a condition worsens, subject to specific rules and deadlines.

## How durations vary by state

Benefit duration is among the most variable features of workers' compensation. States differ in the caps they place on temporary disability weeks, how permanent disability is measured and paid, whether permanent total disability can continue for life, how medical benefits are limited, and whether and how claims can be reopened. Two workers with similar injuries can receive benefits for very different lengths of time depending on where they are employed.

Federal workers' compensation programs administered by the [U.S. Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/workcomp) follow their own separate duration rules that do not govern state systems. Because these timelines are jurisdiction-specific, how long benefits last in any particular claim depends on the governing state law.

## Settlements and how they affect duration

Many workers' compensation claims end not when benefits run out but when the parties agree to a settlement that resolves future benefits. A settlement can change the duration analysis entirely, because it substitutes an agreed resolution for the ongoing stream of benefits the statute would otherwise provide.

Settlements generally take one of two broad forms, though the names and details vary by state:

- **A full and final settlement** — the worker accepts an agreed amount, often as a lump sum, in exchange for closing out some or all future benefits, which may include future medical care.
- **A settlement that leaves medical open** — the worker resolves wage-replacement benefits but preserves the right to future medical treatment for the injury, where the state allows this structure.

Because settlements can permanently affect a worker's rights, states generally require review and approval by the workers' compensation agency or a judge to confirm the terms are appropriate. This oversight is a common feature across jurisdictions, even though the specific standards differ.

Settlements involving future medical care can also intersect with federal programs. When a worker is or may become eligible for Medicare, federal rules may call for the parties to account for future injury-related medical costs so that those costs are not improperly shifted to Medicare. These considerations operate under federal guidance and are separate from state benefit durations.

Because the availability, structure, and approval of settlements are governed by each state — with federal rules layered on for certain medical issues — a settlement can shorten, extend, or convert the duration of benefits in ways that depend on the jurisdiction and the terms the parties reach.

## What typically happens as benefits end

As benefits approach their limits, a claim often moves toward resolution. This may involve a transition from temporary to permanent disability benefits, a return-to-work arrangement, or a settlement that addresses future medical care and wage benefits. Each of these steps is governed by state procedure.

Because the duration of every benefit type is defined by state statute, the practical question of how long benefits last can only be answered by reference to the specific state system handling the claim.
