## Reporting the injury to the employer

For most workers' compensation claims, the process begins with notifying the employer of the injury. States generally impose a deadline for this notice, and reporting promptly is important because a late report can complicate or jeopardize a claim. The notice tells the employer that a work-related injury has occurred and typically starts the formal process.

The required form of notice varies. Some states accept verbal notice, while others call for written notice within a specific number of days. Occupational illnesses and repetitive-stress conditions, which may not have an obvious onset date, are often handled under separate timing rules that run from when the worker knew or reasonably should have known the condition was work-related.

Because reporting deadlines differ by jurisdiction and can be short, the applicable time limit is generally one of the first details that matters in a claim. These deadlines are set by each state's statute.

## Seeking medical treatment

Obtaining medical care serves two purposes: it addresses the injury and it creates documentation linking the condition to work. In many states, the workers' compensation system pays for reasonable and necessary treatment for a covered injury, often without a deductible.

Rules about which provider may deliver that care differ widely:

- **Employer or insurer networks** — some states allow the employer or insurer to direct initial treatment to a designated provider or network.
- **Worker choice** — other states allow the worker to select a treating physician, sometimes after an initial period or with conditions.
- **Emergency care** — emergency treatment is generally available first, with the formal process addressed afterward.

Following the state's rules about treating providers can matter, because care obtained outside the permitted channels may not always be covered.

## Completing and submitting the claim forms

Beyond notifying the employer, most states require specific claim forms to be filed with the employer's insurer or the state workers' compensation agency. A common sequence is that the employer provides a claim form after being notified, the worker completes it, and the employer forwards it to the insurer, which then reports the claim to the state agency.

Once a claim is filed, the insurer generally has a defined period to investigate and either accept the claim, begin paying benefits, or dispute it. If accepted, benefits such as medical care and, where applicable, temporary wage replacement typically follow the state's schedule. If the insurer disputes the claim, the matter may proceed to the state's administrative process.

## How deadlines and procedures vary by state

Filing procedures are highly state-specific. States differ in the deadline to report an injury to the employer, the separate deadline to file a formal claim with the agency, the forms required, the rules for choosing a treating doctor, and the time an insurer has to respond. Some states also have a distinct statute of limitations for filing a claim that is separate from the initial reporting deadline.

For example, [California's Labor Code](https://www.dir.ca.gov/dwc/) generally sets a one-year period for filing a workers' compensation claim, subject to exceptions — a figure specific to California, not a national rule. Other states set different periods, and federal programs administered by the [U.S. Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/workcomp) follow their own procedures that do not govern state claims. Because of this variation, the correct forms and deadlines depend on the state where the worker is employed.

## The employer's and insurer's role after a report

Once a worker reports an injury, much of the process shifts to the employer and its insurer, and their obligations are defined by state law. In many states, the employer is expected to provide the appropriate claim form promptly, report the injury to its insurer, and, in some jurisdictions, file a first report of injury with the state agency within a set period.

The insurer then generally has a defined window to act. Typical steps include:

- **Investigation** — the insurer reviews the reported injury, the medical records, and the circumstances to decide whether the claim is compensable.
- **Acceptance or denial** — within the state's time frame, the insurer either accepts the claim and begins benefits, denies it with a stated reason, or, in some states, pays benefits on a provisional basis while continuing to investigate.
- **Notice to the worker** — the insurer generally communicates its decision and, in the case of a denial, the reasons and the steps for challenging it.

These timelines matter because delays or missed obligations can have consequences. Some states impose penalties on insurers that fail to act within the required period or that unreasonably delay benefits, while employers that fail to report injuries may face their own penalties.

Because the employer's duties and the insurer's response deadlines are set by each state, the pace and structure of this phase vary by jurisdiction. Keeping copies of the injury report, claim forms, and any correspondence is commonly recommended, since this documentation records what was submitted and when — details that can matter if the claim is later disputed.

## What typically happens after filing

After a claim is submitted, the insurer investigates and issues a decision. An accepted claim generally moves into the benefits phase, with medical treatment and any wage-replacement payments provided under the state's rules. A disputed or denied claim generally moves into the state's dispute-resolution process, where the worker may request a hearing before the workers' compensation agency.

Keeping copies of reports, forms, and medical records is commonly recommended because documentation supports the claim at each stage. Since every step is governed by state procedure, the practical path of a claim depends on the jurisdiction and the applicable deadlines.
