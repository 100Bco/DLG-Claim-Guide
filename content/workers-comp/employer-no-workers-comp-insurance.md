## Coverage requirements and the gap when they are unmet

Most states require employers above a certain size or type to carry workers' compensation insurance, or to be approved to self-insure. When a covered employer fails to obtain required coverage, an injured worker faces a gap: the usual source of no-fault benefits — the employer's insurer — does not exist. States have generally built mechanisms to address this situation, because leaving injured workers without recourse would defeat the purpose of the system.

The consequences of being uninsured typically fall on the employer, not the worker. An employer that failed to carry required coverage may face civil penalties, fines, or other enforcement action from the state, and may lose certain legal protections that coverage normally provides. Meanwhile, the state generally tries to preserve some avenue for the injured worker.

Because both the coverage requirements and the remedies for noncompliance are set by each state, what happens when an employer is uninsured depends heavily on the jurisdiction.

## Options that may be available to an injured worker

When a required-to-be-covered employer has no insurance, states commonly provide one or more of the following, though the specifics vary:

- **A state uninsured employer fund** — many states maintain a special fund that can pay benefits to workers injured while working for an illegally uninsured employer, then seek repayment from the employer.
- **A direct claim against the employer** — the worker may be able to pursue benefits or damages directly from the employer, sometimes with the usual legal protections for the employer removed.
- **Loss of the employer's defenses** — some states provide that an uninsured employer sued by an injured worker may not raise certain defenses it would otherwise have.
- **State enforcement** — the agency may pursue penalties against the employer separately from the worker's claim.

Which of these applies, and how a worker accesses them, is defined by state law and procedure.

## When coverage is not required at all

A separate situation arises in the few places where workers' compensation coverage is not mandatory. Texas is the most prominent example: it generally allows private employers to opt out of the workers' compensation system, and employers that do so are known as "[nonsubscribers](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/wc/)." In that setting, the absence of workers' compensation is lawful rather than a violation.

Where an employer lawfully has no workers' compensation, the trade-off shifts. An injured worker may not have access to no-fault benefits, but a nonsubscribing employer may also lose certain legal protections, so the worker may be able to bring a personal injury claim against the employer and, in some cases, benefit from rules that limit the employer's defenses. Because these arrangements are governed by that state's specific statutes, the analysis differs from states where coverage is mandatory.

## How the rules vary by state

This is an area of significant state variation. States differ in which employers are required to carry coverage, whether an uninsured employer fund exists and how it operates, what penalties apply to noncompliant employers, whether and how an injured worker can sue an uninsured employer, and what defenses such an employer loses. A handful of states, and certain categories of employment, fall outside mandatory coverage entirely.

Federal workers' compensation programs administered by the [U.S. Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/general/topic/workcomp) cover their own defined groups and do not fill gaps in state coverage. Because the consequences of an employer's lack of coverage are jurisdiction-specific, the options available to an injured worker depend on the governing state law.

## The employer's exposure and penalties

When coverage is required but absent, states generally direct significant consequences at the employer, both to compensate injured workers and to encourage compliance. The specific penalties differ by jurisdiction, but several types are common.

- **Fines and assessments** — many states impose monetary penalties on employers that fail to carry required coverage, sometimes calculated by the length of the violation or the number of employees.
- **Stop-work orders** — some states can order an illegally uninsured business to cease operations until it obtains coverage.
- **Loss of legal defenses** — a common consequence is that an uninsured employer sued by an injured worker may be barred from raising certain defenses it would otherwise have, which can make it easier for the worker to establish liability.
- **Personal or criminal liability** — in some states, repeated or willful failure to carry coverage can expose owners or officers to personal liability or even criminal penalties.

These consequences reflect a policy choice: the burden of an employer's failure to insure is generally shifted onto the employer rather than the injured worker. The removal of defenses is particularly significant, because it changes the litigation landscape if the worker pursues the employer directly.

Because the penalties, enforcement mechanisms, and effect on an employer's defenses are all set by state statute, an uninsured employer's exposure varies widely by jurisdiction. In states where coverage is mandatory, being uninsured is treated as a serious violation; in the few states or situations where coverage is optional, the analysis is different, as the absence of coverage may be lawful.

## What typically happens next

In practice, a worker injured while an employer lacks required coverage often begins by contacting the state workers' compensation agency, which can explain whether an uninsured employer fund or other remedy applies. The agency may also pursue the employer for penalties and reimbursement. Where coverage was not required, the path may instead run through the civil court system.

Because the existence of a remedy, the role of any state fund, and the ability to sue all depend on the state's statutes, the outcome when an employer has no workers' compensation insurance is determined by the jurisdiction in which the worker was employed.
