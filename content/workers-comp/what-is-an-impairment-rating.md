## What an impairment rating measures

An impairment rating is an estimate, usually expressed as a percentage, of the permanent loss of function that remains after a work injury has stabilized. It is generally assigned once a worker reaches maximum medical improvement — the point at which the condition is no longer expected to improve significantly with treatment. The rating attempts to capture how much lasting impairment the injury has caused.

An impairment rating measures medical impairment, which is not always the same as disability. Impairment generally describes a loss of function of the body, while disability describes how that loss affects the ability to work or perform activities. Many workers' compensation systems use the impairment rating as a starting point and then apply their own rules to translate it into benefits, sometimes adjusting for factors like age, occupation, or earning capacity.

Because impairment ratings carry financial consequences within a claim, the method used to calculate them and the way they convert into benefits are defined by each state and can differ significantly.

## How impairment ratings are determined

Impairment ratings are generally assigned by a physician who evaluates the worker after maximum medical improvement. To promote consistency, many states direct evaluators to use a standardized reference, and states specify which edition or system applies. The evaluation typically considers medical findings, measurements of function, and the nature of the injury.

Common features of the rating process include:

- **A qualified evaluator** — a treating physician or a designated or agreed medical examiner performs the assessment.
- **A standardized method** — states often require use of a particular published guide or state-specific schedule to promote uniform ratings.
- **A written report** — the evaluator documents the findings and assigns a percentage.

When the parties disagree about a rating, most systems provide a way to obtain an additional opinion or to challenge the rating through the state's dispute process.

## From rating to benefits

In many states, the impairment rating feeds into the calculation of permanent disability benefits. A higher rating generally corresponds to greater permanent disability benefits, though the exact relationship depends on the state's formula. Some states use a schedule that assigns set benefit periods to particular body parts or percentages, while others use more individualized approaches.

Because each state applies its own formula, the same impairment percentage can produce different benefit outcomes in different jurisdictions. The rating is often a key input, but it is the state's rules that determine what the rating ultimately means in dollars and duration.

## How rating systems vary by state

Impairment rating is an area of substantial state variation. States differ in which standardized guide or edition they require, how they handle ratings for injuries to specific body parts versus the body as a whole, whether and how they adjust ratings for vocational factors, and how disputes over ratings are resolved. A given injury may be rated somewhat differently, and valued quite differently, depending on the state.

Federal workers' compensation programs administered by the U.S. Department of Labor use their own separate standards that do not govern state claims. Because the rating method and its benefit consequences are jurisdiction-specific, an impairment rating should be understood within the rules of the state where the claim is filed.

## Scheduled and unscheduled injuries

Many state workers' compensation systems draw a distinction that shapes how an impairment rating translates into benefits: the difference between scheduled and unscheduled injuries. Understanding this distinction helps explain why similar ratings can be handled differently even within a single state.

- **Scheduled injuries** — losses involving specific body parts, such as an arm, leg, hand, or eye, that appear on a statutory "schedule." For these, many states assign a set number of benefit weeks to each body part, and the impairment rating adjusts the award within that framework.
- **Unscheduled injuries** — losses not tied to a listed body part, such as injuries to the back, or conditions affecting the body as a whole. These are often evaluated as a "whole-person" impairment and may be handled through a more individualized calculation.

The distinction matters because the same medical impairment can lead to different benefit structures depending on how the state classifies the injury. Some states rely heavily on schedules, while others use whole-person approaches more broadly, and a number of states blend the two.

Whole-person impairment ratings attempt to express how an injury affects overall function rather than a single body part. States that use them often apply additional rules to convert the percentage into a benefit amount and duration, sometimes adjusting for factors such as age or occupation.

Because whether an injury is scheduled or unscheduled — and how each is valued — is defined by each state's statute, the practical effect of an impairment rating depends on both the classification and the state's formula. This is one more reason the same percentage can mean different things in different jurisdictions.

## Why the rating is often contested

Because the impairment rating can directly affect the value of permanent disability benefits, it is one of the more frequently disputed elements of a workers' compensation claim. Disagreements may involve whether the correct method was used, whether the evaluation was thorough, or how the rating should be applied under the state's formula.

For these reasons, states generally provide procedures for second opinions and formal challenges. Since both the rating process and its effect on benefits are set by state law, the significance of any particular impairment rating depends on the jurisdiction handling the claim.
