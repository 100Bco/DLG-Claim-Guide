## Defining maximum medical improvement

Maximum medical improvement, commonly abbreviated MMI, is a medical and legal milestone in a [workers' compensation](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/workers_compensation) claim. It refers to the point at which a treating physician determines that an injured worker's condition has stabilized and is not likely to improve substantially with further medical treatment. In other words, the recovery has reached a plateau.

An important point is that MMI is not the same as a full recovery. A worker can reach MMI while still having lasting symptoms or limitations; the term simply means that additional treatment is not expected to produce significant further improvement. Some workers reach MMI fully healed, while others reach it with permanent effects that then need to be evaluated.

MMI is generally a medical determination made by a physician, but it carries legal consequences within the workers' compensation system. Because those consequences and the procedures for reaching MMI are set by each state, the way MMI functions can differ by jurisdiction.

## Why MMI matters in a claim

MMI often marks a turning point in how benefits are handled. Before MMI, an injured worker who cannot work may receive temporary disability benefits, which partially replace lost wages during recovery. Reaching MMI generally signals that the temporary phase is ending, because the condition is no longer actively improving.

At that point, the focus typically shifts to whether the injury left any permanent effects:

- **End of temporary benefits** — temporary disability payments often stop or change when MMI is reached.
- **Permanent evaluation** — the worker may be evaluated for a permanent impairment, which can lead to permanent disability benefits.
- **Return-to-work planning** — MMI can prompt an assessment of what work the person can perform, with or without restrictions.

Because MMI can trigger a change in benefits, the timing and validity of an MMI determination are sometimes disputed.

## How MMI is determined

MMI is generally assessed by a treating physician based on the worker's medical progress. In many states, if there is disagreement about whether MMI has been reached, the system provides a mechanism to resolve it, such as an independent medical examination or evaluation by a designated or agreed physician.

The evaluation typically considers whether the condition has stabilized, whether further treatment would meaningfully help, and what functional limitations remain. Because the determination affects benefits, states often build in procedures for second opinions or dispute resolution when the parties do not agree.

## How MMI rules vary by state

MMI is used across state workers' compensation systems, but the specifics vary considerably. States differ in who may declare MMI, how disputes over MMI are resolved, whether there are timelines or presumptions about when MMI should occur, and exactly how reaching MMI affects the type and duration of benefits. Some states tie statutory deadlines or caps on temporary benefits to the MMI determination.

Federal workers' compensation programs administered by the [U.S. Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/owcp) use similar concepts but operate under their own separate rules that do not govern state claims. Because the legal effect of MMI depends on the governing statute, the general description here should be confirmed against the rules of the applicable jurisdiction.

## Disputes over reaching MMI

Because reaching maximum medical improvement can change the benefits a worker receives, its timing is sometimes contested. A determination that a worker has reached MMI may end temporary disability payments, which gives both sides a stake in when that point is declared.

- **Concerns about premature MMI** — a worker or treating physician may contend that the condition is still improving and that further treatment could help, so declaring MMI too early could cut off benefits prematurely.
- **Concerns about delayed MMI** — an insurer may contend that a condition has stabilized and that continued temporary benefits are no longer warranted.

To resolve these disagreements, most states provide a mechanism for an additional medical opinion. Depending on the jurisdiction, this may take the form of an independent medical examination, an evaluation by a physician the parties agree on, or an assessment by a doctor the state agency designates for that purpose. The resulting opinion generally helps the agency decide whether MMI has been reached.

The stakes explain why MMI is documented carefully. Because the determination is medical but carries legal and financial consequences, the supporting evaluation typically addresses whether the condition has genuinely plateaued and whether additional treatment would be expected to help. A worker who disagrees with an MMI finding generally may challenge it through the state's dispute process, subject to the applicable deadlines.

Because the procedures for declaring MMI, obtaining second opinions, and contesting the determination are all set by state law, how these disputes are handled varies by jurisdiction. What is consistent is that MMI is treated as a meaningful checkpoint, and its timing can materially affect the course of a claim.

## What typically follows MMI

After MMI, a claim often moves into its final phase. If the injury left no lasting impairment, the claim may resolve with the end of temporary benefits. If permanent effects remain, the next step is frequently an impairment rating — a measure of lasting impairment used in many states to calculate permanent disability benefits.

Because MMI connects the treatment phase to the evaluation of permanent consequences, it is a pivotal point in many workers' compensation claims. How it is reached and what it triggers, however, depend entirely on the rules of the state system handling the claim.
