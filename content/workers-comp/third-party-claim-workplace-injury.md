## What a third-party claim is

A third-party claim in the context of a workplace injury is a personal injury claim brought against someone other than the injured worker's employer. While workers' compensation generally handles the relationship between an injured employee and the employer, it does not necessarily shield outside parties whose negligence contributed to the injury. When such a party exists, the worker may be able to pursue a separate claim against them.

The term "third party" reflects the structure: the first two parties are the worker and the employer within the workers' compensation system, and the third party is an outside person or entity. Because a third-party claim is a fault-based personal injury action, it generally requires proving that the third party was negligent — failed to use reasonable care — and that this caused the injury, unlike the no-fault workers' compensation claim.

Whether a third-party claim is available, and how it relates to workers' compensation, is governed by each state's law, so the specifics vary by jurisdiction.

## Why it can proceed alongside workers' compensation

The exclusive remedy rule generally limits an employee's ability to sue the employer for a workplace injury, channeling that relationship into workers' compensation. Importantly, in most states this limit applies to the employer, not to unrelated third parties. That is why a worker may often receive workers' compensation benefits from the employer's insurer and, at the same time, pursue a personal injury claim against a negligent outside party.

Common examples of potential third parties include:

- **A negligent driver** — when a worker is injured in a vehicle collision caused by someone outside the company.
- **A property owner or another contractor** — when an unsafe condition or another company's conduct on a shared worksite causes harm.
- **An equipment or product manufacturer** — when defective machinery or a defective product contributes to the injury.
- **A subcontractor or vendor** — when an outside company's negligence plays a role.

Whether a particular party can be pursued depends on the facts and on the state's rules, including how it treats certain relationships on multi-employer worksites.

## What a third-party claim can add

A third-party claim can matter because personal injury law may compensate losses that workers' compensation does not. Workers' compensation generally provides medical care and partial wage replacement but does not pay for pain and suffering. A successful third-party personal injury claim, being fault-based, may allow recovery of a broader range of damages, including non-economic losses, subject to proof and state limits.

This potential for broader recovery is a central reason third-party claims are pursued. The trade-off is that, unlike no-fault workers' compensation, the third-party claim requires proving negligence and carries the ordinary risks of civil litigation. ## Reimbursement, coordination, and state variation

A key feature of third-party claims is coordination with workers' compensation, which most states address through subrogation or a lien. Because the workers' compensation insurer has already paid benefits, many states allow that insurer to recover some of its payments out of a worker's third-party recovery, to avoid a double recovery for the same losses. How much can be recovered, and how any remainder is divided, varies by state.

The rules differ in important ways by jurisdiction: which parties count as protected employers, how liens or reimbursement rights are calculated, whether the worker's own share of fault reduces the recovery, and the deadlines that apply. Because these coordination rules and the availability of a third-party claim are set by state law, the interaction between the two systems depends on the governing jurisdiction.

## Common obstacles in third-party claims

Although third-party claims can broaden what an injured worker may recover, they also carry hurdles that workers' compensation does not. These obstacles are shaped by state law and by the nature of the claim.

- **Identifying a viable party** — a third-party claim requires an outside person or entity whose negligence contributed to the injury and who can be held responsible. Not every workplace injury involves such a party.
- **Statutory employer immunity** — on some multi-employer worksites, states extend workers' compensation protection to certain contractors or related entities, treating them like the employer and shielding them from suit. Whether a particular company is protected depends on the state's rules.
- **Proving fault** — unlike the no-fault compensation claim, the third-party claim requires proving negligence, which demands evidence about what the party did and how it caused the harm.
- **Comparative fault** — if the worker is found partly at fault, many states reduce the third-party recovery accordingly, under rules that vary by jurisdiction.

Product-related claims can add further complexity. When defective equipment is involved, the claim may sound in product liability, which has its own standards and defenses. These cases often require technical evidence about the product and how it failed.

Because each of these obstacles is governed by state law — including how immunity, fault, and product claims are treated — the strength and viability of a third-party claim depend heavily on the jurisdiction. The general availability of such a claim does not guarantee that a particular set of facts will support one.

## What typically happens when both claims exist

When a worker has both a workers' compensation claim and a viable third-party claim, the two generally proceed on separate tracks — the compensation claim through the state agency and the third-party claim through the civil courts. The workers' compensation benefits often provide more immediate support, while the third-party claim, if successful, may resolve later and address additional losses.

Because the availability, coordination, and reimbursement rules are all state-specific, how these claims fit together in any particular situation depends on the law of the jurisdiction where the injury occurred.
